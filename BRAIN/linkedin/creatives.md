## The one rule that outranks the rest

> Make the post solve a specific problem for the reader. Then iterate on what works.

Everything below is a default to start from, not a law. The presenter says this himself at the close, right after

## Creative quality hierarchy [deck-sourced]

```
a genuinely good video   >   a decent static image   >   a mediocre video
```

Video is the highest ceiling and the lowest floor. A bad video gets almost nothing, "similar to an ad." A merely
decent landscape photo beats it. **This independently supports brain rule on native video**: Tyler's five native
video posts (median 102) are being beaten by three-week-old text posts, so his current video slot is not clearing
the bar. Either raise the video quality or use a static creative.

## Creative formats that work, ranked

1. **Real photos from your own life, in a professional-looking setting.** The presenter's top recommendation and
   what he mostly uses, because it costs nothing: it comes out of the camera roll. You at a desk, at a laptop, at
   a bookshelf. **The image does not have to relate to the post content.** It works because the platform reads
   the setting as professional and pushes it.
2. **Screenshots as instant proof.** Fastest way to establish that a thing is real. The course uses payout and
   deal screenshots. **Tyler's swap: a terminal mid-run, a working agent's output, a Drive folder that an agent
   actually filled, a repo, a dashboard.** This is strictly stronger for him than a money screenshot, and it does
   not violate the no-money-amounts rule.
3. **Diagrams and infographics.** Simple Canva graphics that illustrate the topic. This is the same slot the
   architecture diagram fills in the build post, which is already a confirmed rule in the brain.
4. **A photo of a handwritten notepad, list, or calendar.** Performs well and the presenter's read is that
   handwriting feels human and real. Novel for Tyler's feed, near-zero effort.
5. **Repurposed YouTube thumbnails.** If a thumbnail earned a high CTR on YouTube, it tends to carry over. Tyler
   already has a thumbnail pipeline, so this is free inventory.
6. **Carousels.** Perform well because advancing a slide is itself an engagement signal. Canva has LinkedIn
   presets. Tyler has `/carousel` already.
7. **Landscape pictures**, especially when the post is anchored to a place.
8. **A photo of the person you are writing about**, for a breakdown post. Well dressed, good light, professional
   setting.

**Every post gets a visual.** No exceptions in the source material.

## The creative does the heavy lifting

Only a couple of lines of text show above the creative in the feed. Same job as a YouTube thumbnail: stop the
scroll and create curiosity. Design the image to earn the "see more" click, not to decorate a post already read.

## Format: the breakdown / study post

**This is the single most transferable format in the video for Tyler, and he has never run it.**

Pick someone or something respected in your space and explain how it actually works. The stated logic:

> break down what an expert does, and people assume you understand it at least as well as they do.

The course uses people (Sam Altman, Dave Ramsey, the Gymshark founder). **Tyler's version is better as
architecture than as personality**, because he is an engineer and can go a level deeper than a marketer can:
how Claude Code's skill system is actually structured, how MCP auth really works under the hood, why a given
agent framework makes the tradeoff it makes. Same mechanism, real expertise behind it, and it stays inside the
positioning rule in `tyler-voice.md`.

Pair it with a photo or logo of the subject. The presenter's read on his own best example is that having a
recognizable face in the image drove a large share of the reach.

## Format: the first-person tactical post (his 10,000-impression example)

Structure, which is worth copying directly:

1. Short imperative hook, 5 words or so
2. A credibility stat from real life ("40+ hours on flights this year")
3. The belief he used to hold, stated plainly ("I thought travel meant lost time")
4. Four or five tactical things he actually does, one line each
5. The principle underneath them
6. A quiet closing line. **No CTA at all.**
7. Landscape photo related to the topic

Note this contradicts the engagement-CTA advice from the same course, and it was his best-performing example
shown. Treat "always add an engagement CTA" as a default, not a rule.

---

## Format: video posts

If posting native video, run **anticipation, then stakes, then reward**: hook with something compelling, build
tension by naming what is at risk, pay it off with the outcome. Professional or animated background. Keep it short.

## Profile plumbing (do this once, it is currently unset)

- **Put what you actually do in the line right under your name.** It renders under your name on every post, so
  every impression carries it.
- **Use the profile's link slot** ("view my services" or equivalent) for the Skool link. This is the clean
  resolution to the link problem: readers can always reach the offer without the post carrying a click CTA.
- Related open item in the brain: Tyler's headline still says JPMorgan Chase while his current company shows
  Pfizer.

## The engagement flywheel, in practice

Already brain rule #11. The mechanics from the video:

- A comment on your post exposes it to that commenter's whole network ("spiderweb networking").
- **Your reply pushes the post out further.** Every unanswered comment is reach left unclaimed.
- A like lets someone reshare in two clicks.
- Engage daily with ~10 accounts that share your audience but do not compete with you.
- **Do not automate this and do not have AI write it.** Reply personally. Delegate only much later, if ever.

## Deliberately rejected for Tyler

- **Money screenshots and money hooks** (payouts, bank balances, "60K in 60 days"). Violates the standing
  no-money-amounts rule and the guru positioning ban in `tyler-voice.md`.
- **Controversy and conspiracy hooks** ("the secret X doesn't want you to know", "you're underpriced by a lot").
  The course recommends them and elsewhere concedes they attract argumentative readers. Use the contrast lane.
- **Client case studies.** Tyler has no coaching clients. His case study is his own system.
- **VA-run engagement.** Wrong stage, and the source says to do it yourself first anyway.
