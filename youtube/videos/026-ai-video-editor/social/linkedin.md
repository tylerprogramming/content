## POST 1 - The story

I stopped editing my own videos.

Not because I got better at it. Because I stopped doing the parts I hated.

Two things about editing made me miserable. Cutting dead air, every pause and um between sentences. And adding word-by-word captions by hand.

Both are completely mechanical. There's no creativity in finding a silent gap or syncing a word to audio.

So I stopped doing them, and started asking AI to do them instead.

Here's the actual workflow.

I open HyperFrames, which is HeyGen's open-source video tool. I drop a raw clip in.

I ask Claude Code to cut the dead air. A three minute clip becomes under two. The only thing removed is the silence.

For captions, I copy a style prompt and ask Claude Code to add it. It transcribes with Whisper and wires the captions on.

If I want a different look, I just ask for another one.

I want to be clear about one thing. I'm not a video editor, and I didn't build a tool. All of this is open-source and free.

And it's not one-click magic. It doesn't replace a real editor. It won't pace your story or pick your best take.

It just does the boring parts. But once the styles are dialed in, it's cheaper and faster than the caption apps I used to pay for.

I didn't get better at editing. I just stopped doing the work a computer should be doing.

## POST 2 - The tactic (no link)

I never learned the video editor.

That sounds like a dodge, so let me explain what I mean.

For a long time the deal with software was simple. Learn the interface, then you get to do the thing. Menus, panels, timelines, keyboard shortcuts. The tool sat between you and what you wanted.

That deal is changing.

The video tool I use now installs a set of Claude Code skills. I don't open a settings panel.

I describe what I want in plain language and it does it.

Cut the dead air out of this clip. Put the pill caption style on it. Actually, try the neon one instead.

That's the whole interaction.

Here's the part I think people are missing. It's not that the software got easier to learn. It's that the interface became a conversation.

I still have to know what I want. I have to know the dead air is what's hurting the pacing, and that the captions need to match my voice.

The judgment didn't go anywhere. The clicking did.

And I don't think this is a video thing. Anywhere you've been paying a learning curve tax to use a tool, that tax is starting to get refunded.

What software have you avoided because the learning curve wasn't worth it?

## POST 3 - The honest take (soft link)

Everyone is posting "AI edited my whole video" right now. Most of it is overselling.

Here's the honest version from someone who actually does it every week.

AI does two things in my editing well. It cuts dead air, and it adds word-by-word captions. Both are mechanical. Both used to eat hours.

That's it. That's the list.

It does not pace my story. It does not know which take is my best one. It does not pick the b-roll or tell me the first sixty seconds are boring.

Every creative call is still mine.

It's also not one click. The clean final looks took real back and forth to dial in the first time.

I keep saying this because I think the overselling is doing damage. People try it expecting magic, get something mediocre, and write the whole category off.

The realistic version is better anyway.

Find the parts of your work that are repeatable and boring. Hand those off. Keep the parts that need your judgment.

For me that was dead air and captions. Once the styles were set, it got cheaper and faster than the apps I was paying for.

What's the part of your work you'd hand off if you trusted it?
