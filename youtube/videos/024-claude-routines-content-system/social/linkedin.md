## POST 1 - The story

My business ran while I was asleep.

Not a metaphor. At 5AM my inbox got triaged and my competitor report got written, and I was in bed.

For a long time my morning was the same routine. Open email, scroll a few hundred messages, work out which five actually needed me, then start writing replies.

By the time that was finished it was 9AM and I hadn't done any real work yet.

Then once a week I'd do the research pass. Open a dozen tabs, look at what everyone else was putting out, take notes, write it up. Another hour, sometimes two.

Both of those were completely mechanical. No decisions in them. Just collecting.

So I moved them off my plate with Claude Routines.

A Routine is a prompt plus your connectors, packaged and put on a schedule. It runs on Anthropic's servers, which means my laptop can be closed and it still fires.

The first one runs at 6AM. It reads my inbox, sorts what matters, and drafts replies to the urgent ones.

The second runs Monday morning. Apify pulls the data, Claude writes the report, and it lands in my Slack before I'm awake.

Building a new one takes about 5 minutes. Write the prompt, add the MCP connectors you need, test it once, put it on a schedule.

And here's the part almost nobody is covering. You can create Routines straight from the Claude Code CLI with /schedule. When you want something running locally while you sit there and watch it, there's /loop.

Cloud for the recurring work. Your own machine for the hands-on work. Same stack, two surfaces.

The thing that actually changed for me wasn't the hours. It was that I stopped skipping the weekly report whenever I got busy.

## POST 2 - The tactic (no link)

Most people pick the wrong task to automate first.

They go straight for the hard one. The thing that takes real thought. Then the output comes back mediocre and they decide this whole thing doesn't work.

The test I use now is simpler than that.

Ask whether the task needs you in the middle of it.

If it does, it isn't a scheduled job. It's a working session, and it belongs on your own machine where you can watch it and steer it.

If it doesn't, if the whole thing is collect, sort, summarize, deliver, then it can run on a schedule without you there at all.

That's the entire split. It also happens to be the split between the two surfaces I use.

Cloud routines run on Anthropic's servers on a schedule, so my laptop can be shut. Local runs happen in my terminal while I'm sitting in front of it.

Same model, same instructions. The only real question is whether I need to be present.

The second thing I'd tell you is about the prompt itself.

Write it like instructions for someone new on your team, not like a question you'd type into a chat box.

Say what to look at, in what order, what to ignore, and what the output should look like when it's right.

A question gets you an answer once. Instructions get you the same answer every Monday.

Then give it the connectors. That's usually where the value actually shows up, because a prompt with access to your inbox and your data does something a prompt on its own can't.

Mine took about 5 minutes to set up and it has run every day since.

What's a task in your week that genuinely doesn't need you present?

## POST 3 - The honest take (soft link)

My automations ran all week while I slept. They did not make a single decision better.

That's a strange thing to admit about something I just made a video on, so let me explain what actually changed.

I run two scheduled Claude routines. One triages my inbox at 6AM and drafts replies to the urgent ones. The other pulls competitor data with Apify and writes a report into my Slack every Monday.

What they removed was the hour of gathering before I could decide anything.

What they did not do is decide anything.

The email routine doesn't know which client is quietly about to leave. It sorts by what looks urgent, and that is not the same thing as what matters.

The report doesn't know which trend is worth my time and which one is noise I've already watched fail twice.

So I still read both. I still change things. Maybe 80 percent of what comes back is right, and the last 20 percent is me.

Honestly, that's the deal with all of this. Never expect it to be a hundred percent accurate. Even at 80 or 90, it needs you to carry it the rest of the way.

The mistake I keep seeing is people expecting the tool to hold the judgment.

It won't, and then they decide the whole category is hype.

The better framing is to find the part of your week that's repeatable and boring, and hand off only that.

Not the hard part. The hard part is usually where your value actually is.

For me it was collecting. For you it might be something completely different.

What's the boring, repeatable part of your week you'd hand off first?
