## POST 1 - The story

I stopped typing prompts into Claude Code.

Not because I got bored of it. Because I figured out I was the slowest part of my own process.

Every morning looked the same. Sit down, type a prompt, wait, read the output, type the next one. Then do the exact same sequence again the next day.

The guy who runs Claude Code at Anthropic said something that stuck with me. He does not prompt anymore. He writes loops that prompt for him. His job is to write the loop.

I am not a developer, and that one line changed how I run my week.

Here is the difference as plainly as I can put it.

A prompt is a vending machine. One coin in, one thing out, then it is over. Tomorrow you walk back up and put another coin in.

A loop is an employee. You tell it what good looks like once, and it keeps working. It checks, it acts, it repeats, until the job is actually done.

The version I like most has a funny name. The Ralph Wiggum loop. It runs the same instruction over and over until the work is finished. Claude Code turned that idea into a plugin called /loop.

So here is my real one.

Every morning at 7, before I am awake, a loop runs the research I used to do by hand and drafts the work that comes out of it.

Then it stops. It does not send anything anywhere. It waits and shows me what it made.

I read it with coffee and approve the good ones. The part of the day I used to dread is already sitting there done.

The thing I did not expect was how much of my week was that. Not hard work. Repeated work.

You do not need to be technical for any of this, which is the part I keep having to convince people of.

If you wrote one loop for your own job, what would you point it at first?

## POST 2 - The tactic (no link)

Do not start with a loop. Start with one good result.

I think this is where most people get stuck with AI automation. They try to automate a task they have never once done well with the tool sitting right in front of them.

So here is the order that actually works for me.

Pick the most boring repeatable thing in your week. Not the hard thing. The hard thing is usually where your value is, and you want to keep that.

Then make Claude do that one thing manually. Once. Sit there and nudge it until you actually like what comes out.

That is the step people skip. You are not automating yet. You are working out what good looks like so you can describe it to something else.

Then write it down. Plain English, the way you would explain it to someone new on your team. Do this first, then this, here is what good output looks like, here is what to avoid.

Now wrap that exact thing in a loop. Every Monday, do this. That is your first loop.

You just stopped being the thing inside the loop.

And then the part that compounds. Every time it does something you do not love, you fix it once and tell Claude to remember it.

Day 30 is genuinely better than day 1, because you are never re-teaching the same correction twice.

You are not building a robot. You are training an employee, one note at a time.

I would not automate everything, to be clear. Most of what I do still needs me sitting there.

But the repeatable parts add up to more hours than you would guess.

What is the thing you do the exact same way every single week?

## POST 3 - The honest take (soft link)

A loop running unattended sounds great right up until you picture it running unattended.

That is the honest tension with this stuff, and I would rather say it out loud than pretend it is not there.

I have loops doing real work while I am asleep. Research, drafts, the boring repeatable middle of my week.

What they do not do is decide anything that matters.

A loop is only as safe as its guardrails, and there are four I will not skip.

It knows the intent. I wrote down what I actually want in plain English, so it is not guessing at my standard.

It has something that can say no. A check, a test, a taste gate. Mine stops and shows me the draft and waits.

It has skills worth calling. The loop is kind of dumb on its own. The real work lives in the instructions you gave it.

And it can always stop. A cap on runs, or a budget, or an expiry. It halts by itself.

Here is the part that surprised me. Those four are not really about safety. They are about trust.

I do not check on the loop anymore, and that only happened because I know exactly where it stops.

The mistake I see people make is handing an agent the whole job and hoping. Then it does something odd once, and they decide the entire idea is broken.

The better framing is smaller. Give it the boring middle of a task. Keep the start and the end for yourself.

That is true whether you make videos, write reports, or clear the same queue every Monday.

What is the part of your work you would never let run without you looking at it?
