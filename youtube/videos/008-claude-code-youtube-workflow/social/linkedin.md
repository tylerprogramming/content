## POST 1 - The story

I run my entire YouTube channel from a terminal.

Not part of it. The whole research and planning side.

For a long time, starting a video meant the same slow routine. Open YouTube, search the topic, click through a dozen videos, take notes on what's working. Then a Google Doc, and outline everything from scratch.

Every new video, the same thing over again. Two or three hours before I even started writing. Half the time I'd skip it and just wing it.

So I built a system. Four Claude Code skills that handle the whole pipeline.

The first one searches YouTube for me. One command pulls the newest videos on a topic and sorts them by views. I see what's working, and what nobody has covered yet.

The second pulls the full transcript of a top video. Then I actually talk to Claude about how it's structured and where the gaps are.

The third takes my topic and my angle and writes the whole package. Titles, hooks, a full script, a filming guide. It even does its own web research first.

The fourth generates the thumbnail.

Here's the part I like. This video was planned by that same system. The script I read on camera came out of it, and so did the thumbnail people clicked to get there.

I want to be honest about what this is. These skills aren't a tool I'm selling. They're just markdown files with instructions.

Any of you can build the same thing for a workflow you repeat every week.

And the best part is it compounds. Every skill I add makes the next video faster.

If you built one skill for your own work, what would you automate first?

## POST 2 - The tactic (no link)

A Claude Code skill is just a markdown file.

That's it. That's the whole thing.

I think people hear "I built four skills" and picture something complicated. Some framework, some setup process, probably code.

It's a text file with instructions in it.

Here's how I made my first one. I picked something I did the exact same way every week. For me it was pulling a transcript off a video and cleaning it up.

Then I wrote down the steps. Not code, just plain English, the way I'd explain it to someone starting on my team. Do this first, then this, here's what good output looks like, here's what to avoid.

Saved it as a markdown file. That's the skill.

Now when I need it, I ask for it by name and Claude does it the same way every time. I'm not re-explaining it. I'm not pasting the same long prompt again.

And it compounds. Every one I write makes the next task faster, because I'm never re-teaching the same thing twice.

The part that surprised me is how low the bar actually is. If you can explain your process to a new hire, you can write a skill.

I'm not saying automate everything. Most of what I do still needs me sitting there.

But the repeatable parts add up to more hours than you'd think.

What's something you find yourself explaining the same way every single time?

## POST 3 - The honest take (soft link)

I automated my YouTube workflow. It did not make my videos better.

That sounds like a strange thing to admit, so let me explain what actually changed.

I built four Claude Code skills that handle my research and planning. They search YouTube, pull competitor transcripts, write the script package, and generate the thumbnail.

What they removed was the two or three hours of setup before I could even start.

What they did not do is make the video good.

The skills don't know which take is my best one. They don't know when a section drags. They can't tell me the first sixty seconds aren't earning the next ten minutes.

All of that is still me.

And I think that's the honest way to talk about this stuff. AI is really good at the mechanical parts of a job, and not good at the judgment parts.

The mistake I see people make is expecting the tool to carry the creative weight. It won't, and then they decide the whole thing is useless.

The better framing is this. Find the part of your work that's repeatable and boring. Hand that off. Keep the part that needs you.

For me that was research and setup. For you it might be something completely different.

These skills are just markdown files with instructions, so you can go build the same thing.

What's the repeatable part of your work you'd hand off first?
