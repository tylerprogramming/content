## Part 3 - Analysis vs Tyler's outlines

### 1. How long are the top courses? Is 6 hours common?

**No. 6 hours is rare and it is an outlier length, not a norm.**

- **Claude Code:** the serious full-course cluster is **3 to 4.5 hours** (Nick 4:10, Albert 3:38, Nick Advanced 3:18, freeCodeCamp 4:27). "Guide" tier is 1-2 hours. Only two pieces exceed 6 hours - Nate Herk's 10-hour course (half of it business/selling) and freeCodeCamp's 12-hour compilation (many instructors stitched together). Neither is a clean single-author product-teaching course.
- **Claude Cowork:** dedicated full courses run **1.5 to 3.5 hours**, and even those are a niche - the traffic all goes to 15-30 minute explainers. Nothing near 6 hours has meaningful traction.
- **Cross-cutting reality:** on both topics the **highest-view content is short** (15-50 min). Length is not what wins views; clarity and a strong title win views. Long courses earn a smaller, more committed audience.

**Standouts to name:**
- Code, most-viewed course: **Nick Saraev, 4:10:43, ~2.2M**.
- Code, longest: **Nate Herk, 10:00** (single author) / **freeCodeCamp, 12:20** (compilation).
- Cowork, most-viewed course: **KJ Rainey, 1:28:07, ~389K**.
- Cowork, longest with traction: **9x, 3:19:49, ~74K**.

### 2. What competitors cover that Tyler's outlines do NOT (gaps to consider)

**Claude Code gaps:**
- **Build-and-sell / client business layer.** Nick, Nate, and Albert all devote huge blocks to finding clients, pricing, cold email, and delivery. This is the single biggest structural difference. It is also **intentional for Tyler to skip** (not a guru / not a growth channel per MEMORY), so treat it as a deliberate non-gap, not a hole to fill. Worth a one-line acknowledgement in the outro ("this is the tool, not a sell-services course") so viewers know it is a choice.
- **Building and deploying a real, deployed product app.** Competitors build actual web apps with payment flows and deploy them (Nick: payment testing + Modal API deploy; Nate: n8n-to-app, RAG, 3D sites). Tyler's capstone is a content-ops helper, which is lighter on "ship a live product with a database and payments." If he wants to close one gap, a real deploy-to-production beat is the highest-value one.
- **RAG.** Nate covers it; Tyler's Code outline does not mention retrieval at all.
- **Agent Teams (multi-agent orchestration).** Nick and Nate both teach "agent teams" as a headline. Tyler covers subagents well but stops short of orchestrating a team of agents. A small nod would match the field.
- **Antigravity IDE.** Nick covers it directly. Tyler names VS Code/JetBrains/Desktop but not Antigravity (he has a separate back-catalog Antigravity video, so this may be intentional routing).
- **Explicit token/cost management framing.** Competitors label a chapter "token management" and "evaluating token usage in MCPs." Tyler covers context management thoroughly but frames it as context, not cost. Adding one honest "what this costs / how to watch usage" beat would match viewer expectations.

**Claude Cowork gaps:**
- **Building skills INSIDE Cowork (no-code).** This is the biggest one. KJ Rainey, 9x, and Ben AI all teach non-coders to create their own Cowork skills, and Ben AI goes deep (skill-building framework, Skills 2.0, A/B testing, eval). Tyler's Cowork outline deliberately routes skill-building to the Code course and keeps Cowork on use cases. Given how central skill-creation is to every competitor Cowork course, consider adding at least one "make a simple Cowork skill without touching code" module - it is a proven expectation for this audience.
- **Vertical use-case blocks (Marketing / Sales / Finance / E-commerce).** Ben AI has full Marketing and Sales sections; KJ has a finance plugin/dashboard; there is an e-commerce-specific course. Tyler has personas (creator / small business / student) which is good, but competitors go deeper into named sales and marketing workflows.
- **"Second Brain" framing.** KJ (Obsidian) and Ben AI both sell the second-brain angle explicitly. Tyler has memory + CLAUDE.md, which is the same substance under a less marketable name.
- **Plugins.** Ben AI and 9x both teach the plugin ecosystem in Cowork. Tyler's Cowork outline covers connectors and custom MCP but not packaging/sharing plugins.

### 3. What Tyler covers that they do NOT (his edge)

- **Honest limits stated out loud.** Nobody else does this seriously: rewind's real limits (bash rm/mv, background subagents, other sessions), the cloud-vs-app-open scheduling caveat, permission precedence (deny always wins), the "magic thinking words are a myth, ultrathink is the real one" correction, "draft only, you hit send." This is a genuine, defensible differentiator in a field full of hype.
- **Permissions / auto mode / allowlist deep dive.** Competitors gloss permissions; Tyler makes it a full lesson (classifier auto-mode, rule syntax, precedence, `/fewer-permission-prompts`). Same for **hooks** - three real hooks (format-on-edit, block .env, notify) where most courses barely mention hooks exist.
- **Context management as its own lesson** with `/rewind` summarize-from-here - more disciplined than the field.
- **Surfaces + teleport** (start on one surface, continue on another, pull it back). No competitor frames the cross-surface handoff this cleanly.
- **Claude Agent SDK build.** Almost no course actually builds a tiny agent on the SDK; Tyler does.
- **Troubleshooting / common-mistakes lesson** with real diagnostic flags (`claude doctor`, `--safe-mode`, `--bare`). Competitors rarely teach debugging your own setup.
- **The "keepable library" payoff** - viewers walk away with real skills/agents they keep, not throwaway demos.
- **Credibility framing** - Fortune 500 software engineer showing "the honest version you can start today." Unique positioning against the make-money-fast crowd.
- **Cowork specifically:** the folder/permission boundary taught as a real safety habit, and the 11-use-case structure so every viewer finds their own job. More rigorous and safer than the "automate anything" competitors.

### 4. Topics no course covers well (open lanes)

- **Honest security/permission model + live troubleshooting of a broken setup.** The whole field skips this. Tyler already leans here; he can own it outright.
- **Cross-surface teleport workflow** as a taught pattern (terminal to web to phone and back).
- **The Agent SDK for small, practical builds** (not a giant agent-engineering course, just "here is how little code reuses the same engine").

- **The cloud-vs-app-open scheduling reality** for Cowork routines - competitors either ignore it or over-promise "runs while your laptop is closed."
- **Genuinely honest limits across the board** - the entire category over-promises; an honesty-first course is a positioning lane, not just a feature.

## Part 4 - Recommendation

### Should Tyler's individual courses stay ~2-2.5 hrs or extend to 4-6 hrs?

**Keep them at ~2-2.5 hours. Do not chase 4-6 hours.** Three reasons from the data:

1. **6 hours is not the norm - it is an outlier, and the outliers win on a different game.** The only courses past 6 hours (Nate 10hr, freeCodeCamp 12hr) get their length from business/selling content or multi-instructor compilation, neither of which Tyler does. The clean single-author product courses top out around 3-4.5 hours. Tyler's Code course at 2:32 is at the short end of the "serious course" band but squarely inside it.
2. **Length does not drive views; clarity does.** On both topics the most-viewed pieces are 15-50 minutes. Extending to 4-6 hours would add production cost and cut completion rates without buying reach. Nick's 4hr course wins on title, promise, and the "build & sell" hook - not on being long.
3. **Tyler's edge is depth-per-minute and honesty, not raw runtime.** His differentiators (permissions, hooks, limits, troubleshooting, SDK) are already denser than competitors. Padding to 6 hours would dilute that.

**Fine-tuning:**
- **Code course:** 2:32 is good. The one worth-considering stretch is a real deploy-to-production build (a live app with a database/payment/deploy step) - that is the single most common thing competitors have that Tyler does not, and it would justify pushing toward ~3:00. Anything beyond 3 hours is not supported by the data unless he adds the business layer, which conflicts with his brand.
- **Cowork course:** 2:15 is already **longer than most Cowork full courses** (the most-viewed dedicated one is 1:28; the 3hr ones barely get traction). Do not extend it. The better investment is adding one no-code "build a Cowork skill" module inside the existing runtime, since skill-building is the one thing every competitor Cowork course teaches and Tyler's currently routes away.

### The 3-course bundle (~7 hrs combined) vs single 6-hr competitor courses

**The bundle is better positioned than a single monolith, and this is a real advantage to lean into.**

- A single 6-hour Nick/Nate course forces a non-coder to sit through terminal and deployment content, and forces a developer to sit through no-code hand-holding. **Tyler's bundle segments by surface and audience** (no-code Cowork / terminal Code / agents), so each viewer gets the ~2-2.5 hour course built for them and can add the others when ready. That is a cleaner product than one 6-hour block.
- **On total depth, ~7 hours across three focused courses matches or beats a single 6-hour competitor course**, while being far easier to navigate, resume, and finish. Completion is the metric that actually converts to community/newsletter signups, and short focused courses complete better.
- **Positioning line:** sell the bundle as "three focused courses, not one endless video - start with the surface you actually use." That directly counters the monolith courses and plays to Tyler's clarity-and-honesty brand.
- One caution: keep the three courses genuinely distinct (Cowork = no-code use cases, Code = terminal/build, third = agents/SDK depth). The main risk in a bundle is overlap; the outlines already avoid it by routing skill-building to Code and use-cases to Cowork - just hold that line.
