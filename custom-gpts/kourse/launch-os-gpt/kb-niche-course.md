## Testing & Completion Before Niche Locking (Video 10)

Before you pick a niche, you need to complete some internal work. Max walks through a testing and self-assessment process to help you find the right fit.

### The "Done is Better Than Perfect" Principle
One of Max's core beliefs: **don't wait until everything is perfect to start**.

Too many people spend months researching the "perfect" niche, the "perfect" course idea, the "perfect" tech stack — and never launch. The real learning comes from doing.

**Max's approach:**
- Pick a direction you're confident about (not perfect about)
- Launch a minimal version
- Iterate based on real feedback
- Perfection is the enemy of done

## Finding Your Niche (Video 11)

### The Apple Strategy for Niche Selection
Apple didn't invent the MP3 player — Creative Lab did. Apple took what existed, refined it, added better UX, and made it iconic with the iPod.

**The principle: Success leaves clues.**

You don't need to invent something new. Find what's already working, and do it better — or for a more specific audience.

**How to apply this:**
1. Look at what's already selling in your space (courses, coaching, communities on Skool)
2. Identify who's doing well and why
3. Find the gap: What are they missing? Who are they not serving?
4. Build your version — better, more focused, or for a more specific audience

### The Ikigai Framework for Niche Selection

Ikigai is a Japanese concept meaning "reason for being." For course/community creators, it helps you find a niche that's sustainable long-term.

Your ideal niche sits at the intersection of four things:

```
         What you're GOOD at
               ↓
What the WORLD NEEDS ← YOUR NICHE → What you LOVE doing
               ↑
         What you can be PAID FOR
```

**Ask yourself:**
1. **What am I good at?** — Skills, expertise, experience. What do people come to you for advice on?
2. **What do I love doing?** — What would you do even if you weren't paid? (Not necessarily your job — could be a hobby or side interest)
3. **What does the world need?** — Is there a real demand? Are people actively searching for solutions to this problem?
4. **What can I be paid for?** — Is there a market willing to pay premium prices for this knowledge?

Your niche should hit all four. If you're good at something and love it but no one will pay for it → hobby, not a business. If people will pay and there's demand but you hate it → burnout within 6 months.

### Common Niche Categories That Work Well
- **Business/Marketing** — How to start, grow, or systemize a business
- **Fitness/Health** — Specific programs (not generic fitness — be specific: "strength training for men over 40")
- **Relationships** — Dating, marriage, parenting
- **Personal finance** — Investing, budgeting, debt payoff, real estate
- **Skills/Career** — Coding, design, writing, sales, public speaking
- **Hobbies** — Golf, cooking, photography, music (people pay a LOT for hobbies)

### Niching Down: The Power of Specificity
"I help people lose weight" → too broad
"I help busy moms over 35 lose 20 pounds without giving up their favorite foods" → specific

**Why specificity wins:**
- Easier to find your audience (you know exactly where they hang out)
- Your marketing message resonates harder ("that's exactly me!")
- Less competition (no one else is talking to exactly that person)
- Higher perceived value ("this is made for ME, not for everyone")

**Max's rule:** Be as specific as you can without making your market too small.

## Course Design Principles (Video 11)

### Course Structure Philosophy
A great course doesn't need to be 50 hours of content. It needs to get students to the result.

**Max's principle:** Design your course backward from the outcome.

1. **Define the transformation** — What is your student's before and after?
   - Before: "I have no idea how to build a course or launch it"
   - After: "I have a live course and my first paying students"
2. **Map the milestones** — What checkpoints do they need to hit to get there?
3. **Build lessons around the milestones** — Each module moves them to the next milestone
4. **Cut everything that doesn't serve the transformation** — If a lesson doesn't directly move them toward the outcome, cut it

### The 5-Module Framework
For most online courses, 5-7 modules is the sweet spot:
- Fewer than 5 feels thin
- More than 10 feels overwhelming (students stall)

**Example structure:**
- Module 1: Foundation/Orientation (mindset, overview, why this works)
- Module 2: Core Skill or System Part 1
- Module 3: Core Skill or System Part 2
- Module 4: Implementation/Launch
- Module 5: Growth/Scaling

### Lesson Length
- Keep individual lessons short: 5-15 minutes max
- Long videos lose attention and feel overwhelming
- It's better to have 10 x 8-minute videos than 2 x 40-minute videos
- Students can better track progress with more frequent completions (dopamine hit)

### The David JP Phillips Presentation Principles
Applied to course video design (same principles from Course OS — Max uses these consistently):
1. **One message per slide** — don't cram everything onto one screen
2. **Use contrast** — make important things stand out visually
3. **Remove distractions** — your audience focuses on you (or the key point), not a busy slide
4. **Tell stories** — stories activate more of the brain than facts alone
5. **Create emotional responses** — people remember how you made them feel

### Planning with Trello
Max uses Trello to plan course content before recording:

**Trello board structure:**
- **Column 1: Module Ideas** — braindump all the content you could teach
- **Column 2: Module Outline** — once you decide on the structure, move cards here
- **Column 3: Script/Notes Ready** — lesson is outlined and ready to record
- **Column 4: Recorded** — video is done
- **Column 5: Published** — uploaded to Skool

Each Trello card = one lesson. The card contains:
- Lesson title
- Key points to cover (3-5 bullet points)
- Any tools or screen shares needed
- Link to the finished video once recorded

## Product-Market Fit: Validating Before You Build (Video 10)

Don't build a full 5-module course and THEN find out nobody wants it. Validate first.

### The Validation Method
1. **Talk to 10 people** — Your ideal customer. Have conversations. Ask about their problems, not your solution
   - "What's the hardest part about [topic]?"
   - "What have you tried? What didn't work?"
   - "If you had a magic solution, what would that look like?"
   - Listen 80%, talk 20%

2. **Pre-sell before you build** — Sell the course before it's finished
   - "I'm building a course on [topic]. It launches in 30 days. I'm looking for 5 beta students at a discounted price in exchange for feedback."
   - If 3-5 people say yes and hand over money → you have product-market fit
   - If nobody bites → iterate the offer before building

3. **Validate the price** — Include price in your validation conversations
   - "If this course existed for $997, would you buy it today?"
   - You're not asking them to buy yet — you're testing price sensitivity

### Signs of Product-Market Fit
- People ask "when is this available?" before you've built it
- You get pre-sales or waitlist signups
- People describe your solution back to you with excitement
- You keep hearing the same problems from different people

### Signs You Haven't Hit It Yet
- People say "that sounds cool" but don't put money down
- You're having to explain a lot why someone needs this
- Low engagement on social posts about the topic
- Nobody's buying similar products from competitors (maybe the market doesn't exist)

## The Identity Shift

Max talks about the "identity shift" that happens as students go through the course:

Before: "I'm someone who wants to build an online course someday"
After: "I am a course creator. I have a live product and paying students."

Your job as a course creator is to architect that identity shift. Every module, every lesson, every live call should move them one step closer to becoming the person they want to be.

This is why community matters so much: surrounding yourself with people who've already made the identity shift accelerates your own transformation. That's the real value of a paid community — not just the content, but the identity reinforcement from peers.

## Copywriting for Course Marketing

Max references Gary Halbert and good copywriting fundamentals for selling your course:

### The Big Three in Copy
1. **Headline** — Stops the scroll. Must speak to the desired outcome or a painful problem
2. **Offer** — What they get + why it's worth the price. Make it feel like a no-brainer
3. **CTA** — Clear, specific, urgent. "Join now" is weak. "Claim your spot today" is better

### Copywriting Framework for Social Posts
- **Hook (line 1):** Controversial statement, question, or bold claim that stops scrolling
- **Problem:** Describe the pain in specific, relatable terms
- **Solution:** Introduce your answer
- **Proof:** One specific result or example
- **CTA:** Tell them exactly what to do next

### Writing in Your Voice
Don't write like a marketer — write like yourself.
- Read your copy out loud. If it sounds weird, rewrite it
- Use the words you actually say in conversation
- Short sentences. Cut 30% of every first draft
- "Done is better than perfect" applies here too — ship it, then optimize

## Handling Common Objections

When someone says they're not sure about joining, here's how Max handles the most common objections:

**"I don't have time right now"**
"Totally get it. But here's the thing — if you don't carve out time now to build this, you'll still be in the same place 6 months from now. The course is self-paced. Even 30 min/day gets you there."

**"I'm not sure this will work for my niche"**
"The systems are niche-agnostic. Whether you're teaching fitness, finance, or photography — the community model and tech stack are the same. What you teach is different, but the business model doesn't change."

**"I've bought courses before and didn't finish them"**
"That's exactly why the community model is different. You're not just getting a course you watch alone — you have accountability from other members, live calls, and a community that's going through it with you. Completely different dynamic."

**"It's too expensive"**
"Let me ask you this — if this course helps you sign even one client, will it pay for itself? Because that's literally the worst-case scenario. One client."
